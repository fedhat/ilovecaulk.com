How to remove old caulk

The unglamorous half of every caulking job, and the half that decides whether the new bead lasts. Here's how to get the old stuff out cleanly, completely, and safely.

By FlavioHow-to · September 2026

Nobody gets into caulk for the removal. But I'll tell you what I tell everyone who asks why their brand-new bead peeled off in a month: new caulk is only as good as the surface under it, and the surface under it is usually covered in old caulk.

Caulk does not bond well to other caulk. Fresh latex over old latex might hang on for a while. Anything over old silicone is a lost cause, because cured silicone is slick, flexible, and chemically unfriendly to almost everything, including new silicone. So the old bead has to go, all of it, and the thin film it leaves behind has to go too. Here's how.

Before you scrape: is your building older than about 1980?

Caulk and glazing compounds used in some buildings built or renovated between roughly 1950 and 1980 can contain PCBs (polychlorinated biphenyls), and some older caulks, putties, and mastics contain asbestos. If you're dealing with old, hard, or crumbly caulk in a building of that era, especially around exterior windows, masonry joints, or in schools and commercial buildings, don't sand, grind, heat, or power-scrape it. Have a sample tested by an accredited lab first. If it tests positive, hire a licensed abatement professional and follow your local and state rules. Pre-1978 painted trim may also carry lead paint, so avoid sanding it and follow lead-safe practices.

First, figure out what you're dealing with

Different caulks come out different ways, so it helps to know which one you've got. Press it with a fingernail and give it a tug.

  • Silicone feels rubbery and slippery, stretches before it tears, and often pulls out in long, satisfying strips. Paint won't stick to it. If it's in a shower, it's probably silicone.
  • Acrylic latex is firmer, sometimes brittle, often painted, and tends to crack and crumble rather than peel.
  • Polyurethane is tough, dense, and stuck on like it means it. Expect to cut, not peel.
  • Butyl and other rubber-based sealants stay gummy and sticky and smear when you scrape them.

Tools that make it easier

  • A utility knife or razor scraper for scoring and slicing.
  • Plastic razor blades for acrylic and fiberglass tubs, shower pans, and anything else that scratches. Steel blades on plastic tubs leave permanent marks.
  • A caulk removal tool with a hooked or triangular head for pulling caulk out of the joint.
  • Needle-nose pliers for gripping and pulling long strips.
  • A caulk softener or remover gel for stubborn beads. They're sold right next to the caulk. Read the label; many are meant for specific caulk types, and they need dwell time to work.
  • A hair dryer or heat gun on low to soften latex. Keep it moving and away from plastics, and never use heat on caulk that might contain PCBs or near solvents.
  • Mineral spirits, isopropyl alcohol, and a non-scratch scrub pad for the residue.
  • Gloves, eye protection, and ventilation, because removers and solvents are not a fragrance.

The removal method

  1. Score both edges

    Run a blade along the top and bottom edges of the bead, holding it nearly flat against the surface so you slice the bond without gouging the tile or tub. On plastic surfaces, use a plastic blade. The goal is to free the bead from its two bonded faces.

  2. Pull it out

    Hook one end with a removal tool, grab it with pliers, and pull slowly at a low angle. Silicone often comes out in long ropes. Latex tends to break into bits, so work it out a section at a time.

  3. Soften what won't budge

    For stubborn sections, apply a caulk remover and give it the full dwell time on the label (often a few hours), or warm latex gently with a hair dryer. Then scrape again. Patience beats force here; force is how tile gets chipped.

  4. Scrape the joint clean

    Get the leftover chunks out of the joint itself, not just the surface. A caulk tool, a stiff plastic putty knife, or an old toothbrush will reach into the corners.

  5. Remove the residue film

    This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most for silicone. Old silicone leaves an almost invisible slick layer. Wipe and scrub the joint with mineral spirits or isopropyl alcohol and a non-scratch pad, then wipe again with a clean rag. Keep going until the surface no longer feels slippery and a drop of water no longer beads up on it. For latex residue, warm water and a scrub brush usually do it.

  6. Deal with any mold

    If there's black mildew in the joint behind where the caulk was, kill it now or it will be back under your new bead. A mildew cleaner or a diluted bleach solution works; the CDC suggests no more than one cup of household bleach per gallon of water for mold cleanup. Let it sit, scrub, and rinse. Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar, or other cleaners.

  7. Dry it thoroughly

    Moisture is caulk's first enemy. Give the joint time to dry completely, ideally a full day in a bathroom, with a fan running. Water trapped in the joint will keep the new bead from bonding and feed the next round of mold.

  8. Final wipe, then recaulk

    Right before you caulk, wipe the joint with isopropyl alcohol and let it evaporate. Then lay your new bead following our step-by-step caulking guide. Don't let a clean joint sit for days, or it'll collect dust and soap all over again.

Surface-by-surface notes

Acrylic and fiberglass tubs and showers

Plastic tools only. Many solvents and removers can dull or craze some plastics, so test anything new on a hidden spot first. Mineral spirits and isopropyl alcohol are generally safe, but check the tub maker's cleaning guidance if you have it.

Ceramic and porcelain tile

Tough and forgiving. Steel blades are fine on the glazed face; just keep them flat so you don't chip the edges or dig into grout.

Natural stone

Marble, limestone, and travertine are sensitive to acids, so skip vinegar and acidic cleaners. Some solvents can stain porous stone too, so test first and use the gentlest option that works.

Windows and exterior trim

Outdoors, old caulk is often hard, cracked, and partly painted over. Score, pry out with a stiff putty knife or a 5-in-1 tool, and brush the joint clean. Look closely at the age of the building here, since exterior window and masonry caulk is exactly where older PCB-containing caulk has been found. See the safety note above.

Kitchen counters and sinks

Grease is the extra villain. After removal, degrease with a kitchen cleaner, rinse, dry, and then do the alcohol wipe. See our kitchen guide for which caulk goes back in.

Rosie's tip

Can't tell whether residue is gone? Sprinkle a few drops of water on the joint. If it beads up like a freshly waxed car, there's still silicone film. Keep wiping.

Cleanup and disposal

Cured caulk scraps can generally go out with household trash, but check your local rules, and treat anything that might contain PCBs or asbestos as a special case. Let rags soaked in mineral spirits or other solvents dry flat outdoors, away from heat and flame, before you throw them out. Rags used with oil-based finishes such as linseed oil can self-heat and catch fire if they're balled up, so never leave those in a pile. Keep removers and solvents in their original containers, capped, and away from kids.

The last word

Removal is slow, fiddly, and totally worth it. Every hour you spend getting a joint truly clean pays you back in years of a bead that stays put. Scrape well, wipe twice, dry completely, and then go have the fun part.
