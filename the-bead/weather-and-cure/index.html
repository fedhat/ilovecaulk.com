Caulking in the cold, the heat, and the humidity

The weather gets a vote on every bead you lay outdoors, and on a lot of the ones indoors. Here's how to read the forecast like a caulker.

By ZoeTechnique · September 2026

Caulk doesn't dry the way paint dries, and it doesn't all cure the same way, either. Some tubes need warm, dry air to do their thing. Others pull moisture out of the air to cure, and are happiest on a muggy afternoon. Use the wrong one on the wrong day and you get beads that sag, bubble, wash out, stay tacky, or never quite stick.

The good news: once you know how your caulk cures, the weather stops being a mystery. The better news: the answer is always printed on the tube. This guide explains what those numbers mean.

Skin, tack-free, paintable, rain-ready, cured

Labels throw a lot of times at you. They mean different things.

Skin time
When a film forms on the surface, often within minutes. You must finish tooling before this. Also called the end of open time. See skinning.
Tack-free
The surface no longer feels sticky and won't pick up much dust.
Paint-ready
For paintable caulks, when you can paint over the bead without ruining it. Latex is often paint-ready in half an hour to a few hours.
Rain- or water-ready
When the bead won't wash out if it gets wet. It is not the same as cured.
Full cure
When the whole bead, all the way through, has reached its final strength and flexibility. This can take days, or longer for thick beads. See cure time.

A bead can be dry to the touch and still soft inside. That's why a joint that "looked fine" tears the first time it's stressed: it was only skin-deep.

How each chemistry cures

Evaporation: acrylic latex and friends

Water-based caulks, including acrylic latex, siliconized acrylic, and elastomeric latex, cure as their water evaporates. That means they like warm, dry, breezy conditions. Cold slows them down, high humidity slows them down, and freezing before they cure can ruin them. It's also why latex shrinks a bit as it dries: the water that leaves takes up space.

Moisture cure: silicone, polyurethane, and hybrids

Silicone, most polyurethanes, and MS polymer hybrids cure by reacting chemically with water vapor in the air. So humidity actually speeds them up, and very dry air slows them down. They cure from the outside in, so a thick bead takes much longer than a thin one. Data sheets for moisture-cure sealants commonly quote cure rates on the order of an eighth of an inch or so of depth per day in mild, humid conditions, and slower when it's cold or dry.

Solvent release: butyl and clear rubber sealants

Butyl and some clear, solvent-based rubber sealants cure as their solvents evaporate. They're often more tolerant of cool weather than latex, but they can stay soft for a long time, they shrink as the solvent leaves, and they need good ventilation.

Caulking in the cold

Cold weather makes caulk thick, slows every kind of cure, and brings the two things adhesion hates most: frost and condensation.

  • Surface temperature is what counts. A wall in the shade can be much colder than the air. Frost or dew on the surface, even an invisible film, will keep caulk from bonding. Ideally the surface should be dry and a few degrees above the dew point.
  • Choose a cold-tolerant product. Most silicones and many hybrids go on in weather that rules out latex. If it's near freezing, latex is the wrong tube.
  • Warm the tubes, not the wall. Keep cartridges indoors overnight, or stand them in a bucket of warm (not hot) water for a while before use. Warm caulk flows and tools far better.
  • Bring more leverage. A higher-thrust-ratio gun helps with stiff material. See the caulk gun guide.
  • Work at midday, when surfaces are warmest and driest, and stop early enough that the bead has time to set before the evening chill.
  • Watch the overnight low for water-based caulks. If it's going to freeze before the bead cures, wait.

Caulking in the heat

Heat is sneakier. Everything seems to go fine, and then the bead skins over mid-tooling, sags out of a vertical joint, or blisters.

  • Sunny surfaces get far hotter than the air, especially dark siding, metal, and roofing. Check the tube's upper limit and remember it applies to the surface.
  • Open time shrinks. Work in shorter runs so you can tool each one before it skins.
  • Follow the shade. Work each side of the house after the sun has moved off it, or save the job for early morning or evening.
  • Polyurethane can bubble in hot, humid conditions, because its reaction with moisture gives off a little gas. Shade, thinner beads, and cooler times of day help.
  • Don't leave tubes in a hot car. Overheated caulk runs, strings, and can start curing in the tube.
Troy's tip

An inexpensive infrared thermometer tells you the actual surface temperature in a second. It's the most useful weather tool a caulker can own, and it's more reliable than guessing by feel.

Humidity, inside and out

Humidity pulls in two directions. On a muggy day, latex can take much longer than the label promises to dry, and it's more vulnerable to rain or condensation while it does. Meanwhile, silicone and polyurethane cure faster. In winter, indoor heating dries the air out, which is why bathroom silicone sometimes takes longer to set in January than in July.

Whatever the chemistry, the joint itself should be dry when you caulk it, unless the product specifically says it can go on damp or wet surfaces. Some roof and hybrid sealants do; most caulks don't.

About "rain-ready"

Claims like "rain-ready in 30 minutes" or "water-ready in 3 hours" mean the bead won't wash out if it gets rained on after that time. They don't mean it's cured, and they usually assume light rain, not a downpour or water pooling on the bead. Check the forecast. For water-based caulks outdoors, aim for a comfortable dry window after you finish, ideally a full day, rather than cutting it close to the label.

The best time of year to caulk

Here's the part most people never hear. Joints change width with temperature. Materials expand in heat, so joints are at their narrowest on a hot day, and contract in cold, so joints are at their widest in winter.

If you seal a joint on a scorching day, it's already squeezed tight, and all its future movement is stretching as winter pulls it open. Seal it in deep cold, and it's at its widest, so all its future movement is compression. Neither is fatal with a good sealant and a well-designed joint, but a bead laid in mild weather sits in the middle of its range and splits the difference. That's one more reason spring and fall are caulking season. (Joint design matters too; see backer rod and the art of joint design.)

A quick weather checklist

  • Is the surface temperature inside the tube's application range?
  • Is the surface dry, with no frost or dew?
  • Will it stay dry, and above freezing for water-based caulk, until the bead can take it?
  • Is the sun off this side of the house?
  • Did the tubes spend the night somewhere comfortable?

Five yeses and you're good to go.
