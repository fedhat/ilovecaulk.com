How to lay a perfect bead

Fourteen steps, one steady hand, and zero mystery. This is the whole method we use on every joint in the house, from the tub to the trim.

By RosieHow-to · September 2026

A good bead of caulk is almost invisible. It's a clean, even line that looks like it grew there, keeps water and air where they belong, and doesn't draw the eye. A bad bead is the opposite: lumpy, smeared, gapped, or already peeling. The difference between them is rarely talent. It's almost always prep, the right tube, and a handful of habits that nobody tells you about until you've already made the mess.

So here they are, all of them, in order. Read it once before you start, then keep it open on your phone while you work. We won't judge. We will, however, judge you if you skip step two.

What you'll need

  • The right caulk for the joint. Wet areas get 100% silicone; painted trim gets acrylic latex; outdoors gets something built to move. If you're not sure, the Caulk Finder will tell you in about a minute, and our guide to caulk types explains why.
  • A decent caulk gun. A dripless, smooth-rod gun is worth the few extra dollars. More on that in our caulk gun guide.
  • A utility knife (for cutting the nozzle and scoring old caulk) and a plastic scraper or caulk removal tool.
  • Isopropyl alcohol and lint-free rags for the final wipe-down.
  • Painter's tape (optional, but a gift to beginners and perfectionists alike).
  • Backer rod if any gap is deep or wide.
  • A tooling tool: a gloved finger, a plastic caulk-finishing tool, or the back of an old spoon.
  • Nitrile gloves, paper towels, and a trash bag within arm's reach. You will need them sooner than you think.

The method, step by step

  1. Choose your caulk before you choose your joint

    Ask three questions: will it get wet, will it move, and will you paint it? A bathtub seam is wet and moves a little: 100% silicone. Baseboard-to-wall is dry and gets painted: acrylic latex. Siding and window trim outdoors move with the seasons: an elastomeric, polyurethane, or hybrid sealant. Check the date on the tube, too. Old caulk can refuse to cure.

  2. Remove every bit of the old caulk

    New caulk does not bond well to old caulk, and almost nothing bonds to old silicone. Cut it out, peel it out, and scrape off the residue. We wrote a whole article on removing old caulk because this is where most jobs are won or lost.

  3. Clean it, then dry it completely

    Dust, soap scum, grease, and mold all get between caulk and the surface and ruin adhesion. Clean the joint, kill any mildew, then let it dry fully. In a bathroom that can mean waiting a day after the last shower. Finish with a wipe of isopropyl alcohol and let it flash off.

  4. Tape the edges (optional, highly recommended)

    Run painter's tape along both sides of the joint, leaving the gap plus a hair on each side exposed. Tape gives you perfectly straight edges and contains the smear when you tool. It's especially helpful with silicone, which is slippery and stubborn to clean up.

  5. Fill deep gaps with backer rod

    Caulk is not a gap filler for deep holes. If the joint is deeper than about 1/2 inch, or wider than about 1/4 inch, press in foam backer rod so the caulk ends up roughly half as deep as it is wide. Rod also stops the bead from bonding to the back of the joint, which lets it stretch. The why and the how are in our backer rod guide.

  6. Cut the nozzle small, at about 45°

    Use a sharp knife, not the hole in your gun's handle, which tends to crush the tip. Cut at roughly a 45° angle and start smaller than you think: a hole about 1/8 inch across suits most household joints. You can always cut more. You can never cut less.

  7. Puncture the inner seal

    Most cartridges have a foil seal at the base of the nozzle. Push the gun's built-in poker wire (or a long nail) all the way down the nozzle to break it. Skip this and you'll squeeze harder and harder until the back of the tube gives out instead.

  8. Load the gun and prime it

    Press the release, pull the plunger rod all the way back, drop in the cartridge, and push the rod forward until the pusher plate meets the tube. Give the trigger a few gentle squeezes over a paper towel until caulk just reaches the tip.

  9. Pick push or pull

    Hold the gun at about 45° to the joint. Pulling the gun toward you, so the bead trails behind the tip, is easier to control and the best choice for visible interior work. Pushing the tip ahead of the bead forces caulk deeper into the joint and is favored for exterior joints that need full contact. Either works; the key is to commit to one per run.

  10. Move at a steady speed with steady pressure

    This is the whole game. Squeeze gently and continuously and move just fast enough that the bead fills the joint and touches both sides. Keep the tip in light contact with the joint. Work in runs you can finish comfortably, about two to three feet at a time for fast-skinning silicone, and try not to stop in the middle of a run.

  11. Release early

    Caulk keeps flowing for a moment after you let go. Ease off the trigger a couple of inches before the end of the run and let the pressure carry you home. With a ratchet gun, press the release plate the instant you stop, or the tube will keep oozing onto your floor.

  12. Tool the bead while it's still wet

    Every caulk has an open time before it skins over, often somewhere between a few minutes and half an hour. Within that window, run a tooling tool or a gloved fingertip along the bead in one smooth pass. Tooling presses the caulk into the joint and leaves a slightly concave surface. For latex, dampen your finger with water; for silicone, a light mist of soapy water on the tool (not in the joint) keeps it from sticking. Wipe the excess onto a paper towel after each pass.

  13. Pull the tape immediately

    Don't wait for it to cure. Peel the tape off right after tooling, pulling away from the joint at a low angle so the bead's edge stays crisp. If a little lip forms, one very light pass with a wet finger will soften it.

  14. Let it cure, really cure

    Skin-dry is not cured. Latex caulks are often paintable within half an hour to a few hours; most silicones want about 24 hours before they see water unless the tube says otherwise; thick beads take longer. Keep the area dry, keep pets and elbows away, and read the cure time on the tube. Our weather and cure guide explains why a humid day speeds up silicone but slows down latex.

Flavio's tip

Caulking a bathtub? Fill it with water first and leave it full until the caulk cures. The weight of the water opens the joint to its widest position, so the bead won't be stretched and torn the first time you take a bath.

A few habits that separate good from great

Practice on cardboard

Before you touch the real joint, run a couple of beads along the fold of a cardboard box. You'll learn how fast your gun delivers, how the caulk behaves, and how much pressure you need. It takes two minutes and saves a redo. (Or play Bead Master, which is the same idea with a scoreboard.)

Keep the bead as small as the joint allows

The most common beginner mistake is too much caulk. A big bead doesn't seal better; it just smears wider, shrinks more, and looks worse. The joint should be filled and the edges should be touched. That's it.

Do corners as two runs

At an inside corner, stop a hair short, then start the next run from the corner going outward. Tool both runs together so they blend. Trying to turn a corner mid-bead usually leaves a blob.

Park the tube properly

When you're done, release the pressure, wipe the tip, and plug it with a nail, a screw, or a purpose-made cap. Wrap the nozzle in tape or plastic. A capped tube can keep for weeks; an open one turns into a very expensive plug overnight.

Karen's tip

On painted trim, caulk first, paint second. Paint hides a latex bead beautifully. Caulk over fresh paint just draws a line around every flaw.

Troubleshooting

If a bead went wrong, it almost always went wrong for one of these reasons.

Common caulking problems, causes and fixes
What you seeLikely causeWhat to do
Lumpy, wavy beadUneven speed or pressure; stopping mid-runTool it smooth while wet. Next time, shorter runs and a steady pace.
Caulk keeps oozing after you stopA ratchet gun still holding pressurePress the release plate every time you stop, or switch to a dripless gun.
Gaps and air pockets in the beadMoving too fast, nozzle hole too big, or a deep joint with no backer rodCut a smaller tip, slow down, try pushing instead of pulling, and fill deep gaps with backer rod.
Edges peeling awayDust, moisture, soap, or old silicone residue on the surfaceCut it out, clean with isopropyl alcohol, dry completely, and redo.
Cracks after a few monthsA low-movement caulk in a moving joint, a bead that's too thin, or three-sided adhesionSwitch to a higher-movement sealant and use backer rod or bond-breaker tape.
Bead sank into a gullyNormal shrinkage of water-based latex, especially in wide gapsApply a second thin bead once the first has cured, or choose a low-shrink formula.
Silicone still tacky days laterAn expired tube, a very thick bead, or very cold, dry airGive it time and airflow. If it never firms up, remove it and use a fresh tube.
Paint beads up or peels off the caulkIt's siliconeSorry. Remove it and use a paintable caulk.
Mildew back within weeksMold left in the joint, or a bathroom that never dries outSee why shower caulk turns black.

The last word

Perfect beads come from boring virtues: clean surfaces, the right tube, a small nozzle, steady hands, and patience while it cures. Do those things and your caulk will quietly do its job for years, which is the highest compliment a bead of caulk can receive.
